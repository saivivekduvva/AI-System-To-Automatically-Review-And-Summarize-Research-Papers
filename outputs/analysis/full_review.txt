## TASK 2: REFINED SYNTHESIS

### Refined Abstract

Generative Artificial Intelligence (AI), particularly Large Language Models (LLMs), is increasingly being explored for deployment in safety-critical domains, such as software safety engineering, where rigorous reliability and harm prevention are mandatory. This application necessitates outputs of verifiable quality, indicating that unguided, raw LLM deployment is insufficient for meeting stringent safety requirements. This paper investigates advanced methodological frameworks required to manage the complexities and inherent safety risks associated with integrating generative AI into formal hazard analysis processes. Our findings reveal that while LLMs can process structured formats (e.g., XML), performance and analytical accuracy are significantly enhanced when system descriptions are presented in a format closely aligned with natural language semantics. Furthermore, the study identifies substantial risks associated with equipping agents with multiple external tools, emphasizing the critical need for linguistically distinct trigger conditions to prevent erroneous tool invocation. Ultimately, the research validates the necessity of employing sophisticated, guided approaches—specifically structured Chain-of-Thought (CoT) flows—to ensure accurate task formulation and reliable information retrieval, while maintaining the non-negotiable requirement for expert human oversight in validating all AI-generated analytical suggestions.

---

### Refined Methodology

This study investigates the application of transformer-based Large Language Models (LLMs) to structured, safety-analytical tasks, specifically focusing on reasoning about fault propagation within model graphs and associated hazard identification. The core methodological challenge addressed was adapting these models, typically utilized for general text generation, to the rigorous demands of formal hazard analysis.

The experimental design incorporated an analysis of input data representation. We tested the LLMs’ capability to interpret structured formats, such as XML, against descriptions rendered in a more natural language-centric format. A key observation was that operational accuracy improved when the input system description favored semantic richness over strict syntactic adherence, suggesting a preference for linguistically accessible input during prompt engineering for safety analysis.

A secondary methodological component involved equipping the LLM agents with multiple external tools necessary for complex reasoning chains. A significant constraint identified was the sharp decline in invocation accuracy when multiple tools were available, unless the verbalized trigger conditions for each tool were sufficiently orthogonal in the linguistic space. To counteract this ambiguity, the research employed a highly structured, constrained Chain-of-Thought (CoT) flow. This flow was engineered to tightly couple tool invocation triggers to specific, predefined task concepts, thereby enforcing precision, preventing linguistic overlap, and ensuring the correct tool was selected for each required step across the analytical sequence.

---

### Refined Results

The empirical results confirm that deploying LLMs in safety engineering necessitates significant methodological refinement beyond basic prompting techniques. A primary finding concerns input format sensitivity: direct interpretation of strictly structured data (e.g., XML) introduced measurable inaccuracies, whereas providing system descriptions closer to natural language semantics substantially mitigated these errors. This outcome suggests that the LLM’s underlying semantic processing capabilities are more robustly engaged by linguistically rich inputs during safety reasoning tasks.

A critical finding emerged regarding multi-tool utilization. Equipping an agent with a suite of functional tools introduced a high probability of erroneous invocation, directly correlated with the degree of linguistic overlap between the defined tool trigger conditions. When triggers shared significant linguistic features, the agent demonstrated unreliable selection capabilities.

The implementation of a highly structured, concept-guided Chain-of-Thought (CoT) flow proved effective in resolving these constraints. By rigorously associating tool triggers with distinct, predefined task concepts, the methodology achieved precise and non-overlapping tool invocation across all tested scenarios. This structured guidance successfully ensured accurate task identification, correct formulation of intermediate reasoning steps, and reliable retrieval of necessary information via the appropriate tools. These results establish a viable pathway for automating aspects of hazard analysis, contingent upon maintaining tight procedural control and expert human validation of all AI-derived analytical conclusions.
